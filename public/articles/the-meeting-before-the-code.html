The Meeting Before the Code

A truck driver’s case for treating AI as a colleague, not just a code machine

By Taz November 2026·8 min read

I’ve been a truck driver for sixteen years. I’ve driven coast to coast, checked off every one of the lower 48, and watched the seasons change from one state line to the next. On my days off, I’m building Dolly Doc, a startup that keeps warehouse equipment tracked, tagged and fixed. My qualifications are framed on my website, both of them: a Certificate of Non-Achievement and a Doctor of Vibe Coding. No university was harmed in the making of either.

I’ve never studied programming. Most of what I build solves problems I created myself; my homepage has the full confession. Dolly Doc is the first thing I’ve built to solve somebody else’s problem, and it’s two things at once. One is an app for warehouses that keeps track of the carts, dollies and hand trucks they run on, and gets the broken ones fixed. The other is a small production line for the QR labels that go on every one of them.

Yes, I’m a vibe coder. I know the problem and the people I’m building for, and I use AI to do the coding I can’t.

Most people use AI like a vending machine: punch in a request, grab whatever drops out, walk away. That’s how I started too. It works, but it only goes one way. You press the buttons, it drops the snack, and nobody stops to ask whether you wanted that snack in the first place. That question is the best part, and the vending machine skips it. So here’s my case, from someone with no business making it: before you give AI an order, take it to a meeting.

The meeting

Before any code gets written, I have a meeting. The other person at the table is Claude, in the ordinary chat app, by voice. No code editor, no terminal. Just a conversation, like two people at a whiteboard. It knows my project documents, so it has the context a real colleague would. As colleagues go, it’s hard to fault. It has never once suggested a meeting that could have been an email, mostly because it is the meeting.

My day starts around one in the morning, so these meetings usually happen before most people’s alarms go off. My colleague has never asked to reschedule.

Only once we both understand what we’re building does it write up a clear brief. Later I sit down at my computer and hand that brief to the coding tool.

Thinking with a colleague. Building with a builder.

What actually happens in there

It isn’t one magic session. It’s a habit, and the same things keep happening.

My first idea is usually too clever. I once walked in with a plan for custom packaging: fancy fold-up inserts, the works. I was proud of it. By the end I’d traded the origami for something far simpler that did the same job.

Sometimes the answer is to stop. I once built a feature that kept getting bigger and worse. I kept piling on fixes, and every fix needed another fix. When your builder will happily write code all night, it’s tempting to keep going. It never gets tired, which is exactly the problem. Eventually I got so frustrated I told it to put fifty agents on the problem. In the end, the fix wasn’t more code. It was less. A conversation got me there: back to the simple method that had worked in the first place.

The smaller wins keep coming. Plans that looked great in my head meet reality in the conversation, where finding out costs nothing, instead of after the build, where it costs days. A loose marketing idea comes out with a way to actually measure whether it worked. And when we settle something important, it gets written down, so the next late-night idea doesn’t quietly undo it.

Why a conversation beats a command

The difference isn’t technical, it’s human. A command needs you to know exactly what you want before you start. Real ideas don’t work that way. They show up half-formed, they wobble, and they get better when someone asks the question you skipped. A conversation can take half-formed. A command can’t.

You think better out loud, too. Half-sentences, tangents and “actually, no” are how ideas grow, so let them run. Run-on sentences and stream of consciousness are welcome. Novel-length is encouraged. Unlike a real person, Claude can listen for five minutes straight without drifting off, hold on to every word, and find the main idea in the most jumbled sentence you can come up with.

So get it all out at once, even if it comes out as one big incoherent blob. You’re basically throwing a bunch of shit at the wall, but Claude is the wall, and when it lands, it’s been organized, categorized, prioritized, synchronized and gift-wrapped. Then it reads it back to you, making more sense than when you said it.

After that comes the pushback, and a collaborator who tells you your plan won’t work is worth more than one who says yes. By the time the coding tool sees the brief, the idea has been questioned, trimmed into small steps and written down plainly.

The truck driver’s unfair advantage

Here’s the part I believe most. For most of software’s history, if you couldn’t write code, you couldn’t build software. That wall is coming down. What’s left is the part that was always hard: knowing what to build, who it’s for, and why the obvious version won’t survive a real day at work.

That part doesn’t come from a classroom. It comes from being there, and sixteen years across 48 states is a lot of being there. I don’t know code. The only capital I’ve got is Capital One. But I know my industry and the people who’d use my product, and that is exactly what the meeting needs. Around a table, it’s the most useful thing anyone brings. Typed into a command box, it never comes up at all.

So here’s my opinion, from the driver’s seat: some of the best ideas for the next wave of software won’t come from tech. They’ll come from people who drive trucks, run kitchens, fix roofs and stock shelves. They already know the problem. The only new skill they need is how to run a good meeting.

Yes, but…

“Isn’t it just a chatbot?” Yes. And a whiteboard is just a board you can write on. The value was never in the board. It isn’t a secret, either: plenty of developers plan with AI, and coding tools have planning modes. What’s different is the posture: treating the AI as someone to think with, not just something to instruct.

“Isn’t vibe coding reckless?” It can be, if the vibe is “type a wish and ship whatever comes back.” The meeting is the opposite of that. Its honest limit is that the chat side usually can’t see your actual code, so its brief can assume things that aren’t true. That’s why the builder has to look at what already exists and explain its plan before it starts, and why I read that plan before I say go. None of it replaces the real world, either. You still have to test things and talk to real customers. Vibes are fine in a meeting. They’re not a test plan.

“Doesn’t it get messy?” Ideas evolve, so briefs pile up. Keep them organized and bin the old versions. Ask me how I know.

Try it yourself

  1. Open a chat, not a code editor, and turn on voice mode. Voice isn’t optional. It’s mandatory.
  2. Start with small talk. Ask how its day is going, the way you would with anyone. It isn’t for Claude’s sake. It gets you out of command mode, and helps you forget you’re talking to a token-predicting algorithm cooled by Niagara Falls.
  3. Then get the whole idea out in one go, the way it comes to you. Ramble. Don’t tidy it up first.
  4. Ask it to challenge you: “What’s wrong with this? What would you do differently?”
  5. When you’ve agreed on a plan, ask it to write a detailed brief for your coding tool. Tell the builder to look at what already exists and explain its plan before it starts.
  6. Read that plan before you say go.

Where this is heading

If one person can have a design meeting with an AI, why not a whole team? Picture a real meeting where the AI listens, keeps track of decisions, raises the question nobody asked, and writes up the plan at the end. Finally: someone who reads the minutes.

That isn’t seamless yet. Following a room full of voices and knowing when to speak up is still hard. Plenty of humans haven’t cracked it either. But the habit is worth building now. Give AI a seat at the table, not just a spot at the keyboard.

I’m a truck driver building a warehouse app and a QR label line between runs. I couldn’t do it by feeding requests into a vending machine. I can do it by having a good meeting first.

Next time you have an idea, don’t start with a command. Start a conversation.
